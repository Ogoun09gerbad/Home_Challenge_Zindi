## Which pseudo-labeling / semi-supervised learning techniques are documented as effective in 2025-2026 on tabular data with a rare target, and with what precautions?

### Takeaway
Pseudo-labeling remains a standard technique among Kaggle Grandmasters in 2025-2026 (formalized in NVIDIA's "Kaggle Grandmasters Playbook"), with three documented precautions: (1) use probabilistic/soft labels rather than hard ones, (2) recompute pseudo-labels per fold in K-Fold to avoid validation leakage, (3) finish with fine-tuning on the original labels only. On Zindi, the legality of this practice is explicitly disputed and depends on the exact rules of each competition — Zindi staff have in the past forbidden using the test set to build a model, threatening to disqualify the winning solution.

### Cited Findings
- The "Kaggle Grandmasters Playbook" (NVIDIA Developer Blog, co-written by Chris Deotte, Kazuki Onodera, Théo Viel, Mrigendra Agrawal) describes pseudo-labeling as: train a "Level 0" model, use it to predict labels on unlabeled data (test set or external data), then train a "Level 1" model on the union of original + pseudo-labeled data — [NVIDIA Developer Blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data)
- The same article states the principle: "The stronger the model, the better the pseudo-labels" (the stronger the teacher model — notably via ensembles or multiple rounds — the better the pseudo-labels) — [NVIDIA Developer Blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data)
- Explicit recommendation to use probabilities (soft labels) rather than hard 0/1 labels, to reduce the noise introduced and to allow filtering out low-confidence samples — [NVIDIA Developer Blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data)
- Pseudo-labels can be used for pre-training, followed by a final fine-tuning on the original labeled data only (last step of the pipeline) — [NVIDIA Developer Blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data)
- Explicit anti-leakage precaution: "if you use K-Fold or Nested K-Fold, you can make this generalize better and avoid leakage" — in other words, compute K sets of pseudo-labels (one per fold), so that a fold's validation data never receives pseudo-labels from a model that has seen it — [NVIDIA Blog, Chris Deotte, cuML stacking](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/grandmaster-pro-tip-winning-first-place-in-a-kaggle-competition-with-stacking-using-cuml/)
- BirdCLEF 2024 (example cited in the playbook, not tabular but same logic) used soft pseudo-labels on unlabeled audio clips to improve generalization to new species/recording conditions — no figures given — [NVIDIA Developer Blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data)
- Pseudo-labels can also be used to spot noisy training samples (doubtful labels), for example where several models disagree — [NVIDIA Developer Blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data)
- On Zindi, official staff explicitly forbade (in at least one competition, the SDG Text Classification Challenge) using the test set during training: "please note that you should not use the test set to help build your model! Bring in the test data only at the end to generate your submission", threatening to remove a winning solution that did so — [Zindi SDG discussion](https://zindi.africa/competitions/sustainable-development-goals-sdgs-text-classification-challenge/discussions/38)
- In another Zindi competition (Gender-Based Violence Tweet Classification Challenge, 2021), a moderator account replies that using the test set is not against the rules and that "pseudo labeling is a technique widely used in data science competitions" — but another participant in the same thread calls it "cheating" ("not using the test set, that is cheating") — a contradiction left unresolved in the thread, a sign that the rule depends heavily on each challenge's specific rules — [Zindi GBV discussion](https://zindi.africa/competitions/gender-based-violence-tweet-classification-challenge/discussions/9122)

### Inferences
- For the target competition (85/15, 10 folds × 7 seeds bagging already in place), the "K pseudo-label sets per fold" precaution is directly compatible with the existing CV infrastructure: it would suffice to generate the test pseudo-labels inside each training fold (not a single global pass), so that the uncertainty of the pseudo-labels is not optimistic.
- Given the 85/15 ratio and the combined logloss/AUC target, aggressive pseudo-labeling on test points close to 0.5 (uncertainty zone) is risky: these are precisely the points where the Gaussian label noise documented in the context could dominate, so the net gain from pseudo-labeling could be small or negative if not properly filtered by confidence.
- Before investing in this technique, it is imperative to reread the exact rules of the targeted Zindi competition (not found in this research): the SDG thread shows that Zindi has historically penalized this practice in some challenges, while another thread tolerates it. This is a regulatory concern, not just a technical one.

## Which level-2 stacking/blending techniques have shown a documented net gain recently?

### Takeaway
The best-documented source (the Kaggle Grandmasters Playbook, 2025) describes two techniques beyond a simple weighted blend: **hill climbing / forward selection with replacement** (equivalent to Caruana et al. 2004) to choose blend weights by greedy selection, and **multi-level stacking** where the out-of-fold (OOF) predictions of level-1 models become features of a level-2 model (GBDT + NN recommended in parallel), with a level 3 that averages the level-2 outputs. A March 2026 winner pushed this logic to 4 levels and 150 models selected from 850 experiments.

### Cited Findings
- **Hill climbing**: start from the best single model, iteratively add other models with different weights, keep only the combinations that improve validation, repeat until stagnation; GPU acceleration (CuPy) makes it possible to evaluate thousands of weight combinations in parallel (vectorized RMSE/AUC) — [NVIDIA Developer Blog, Playbook](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data)
- The winner of the "Calorie Expenditure" competition (cited in the playbook) blended XGBoost, CatBoost, neural networks and linear models with this hill climbing method — [NVIDIA Developer Blog, Playbook](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data)
- Concrete figure cited: for the "Fertilizer" challenge, the ensemble of 100 XGBoost seeds stabilizes the score around 0.379 MAP@3, versus about 0.376 for the average single seed — a net gain of about +0.003 just from seed aggregation, consistent with the order of magnitude sought in the user's context — [NVIDIA Developer Blog, Playbook](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data)
- **3-level formalized stacking** (winning solution of "Podcast Listening Time", Playground series, by Chris Deotte): Level 1 = diverse models (GBDT, NN, SVR, KNN, Random Forest, MLP, TabPFN, XGBoost, LightGBM — up to 75 models chosen from 500 experiments); Level 2 = models trained on Level 1 OOF predictions as features, selected by **forward feature selection**, with derived features such as `confidence = std(OOF)` and `consensus = mean(OOF)`; recommendation to have at least two model families at Level 2 (a GBDT and an NN); Level 3 = weighted average of Level 2 outputs — [NVIDIA Developer Blog, cuML stacking](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/grandmaster-pro-tip-winning-first-place-in-a-kaggle-competition-with-stacking-using-cuml/)
- Result of this solution: cross-validation RMSE 11.54 vs private leaderboard RMSE 11.44 (small gap, consistent between CV and private LB) — [NVIDIA Developer Blog, cuML stacking](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/grandmaster-pro-tip-winning-first-place-in-a-kaggle-competition-with-stacking-using-cuml/)
- More recent evolution (Playground competition, March 2026, telecom churn scored on AUC): winning solution = **4-level stack, 150 models selected from 850 experiments**, built by LLM agents (GPT-5.4 Pro, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.6) following the Playbook workflow (EDA → baselines → feature engineering → hill climbing → stacking), more than 600,000 lines of generated code — [NVIDIA Developer Blog relayed by blockchain.news / Quantum Zeitgeist](https://quantumzeitgeist.com/nvidia-agents-kaggle-competition-generate/)
- The **greedy selection with replacement** method ("greedy forward selection with replacement", equivalent to Caruana et al. 2004, "Ensemble Selection from Libraries of Models") works as follows: start from the best-scoring single model; at each round, for each candidate, form an equal-weight average (current ensemble + candidate), keep the candidate only if it improves the score; the same model can be selected several times (equivalent to weight optimization); a model's final weight = its number of selections / total number of rounds — requires at least 3 diverse OOF models as input — [SKILL.md claudskills.com, citing Caruana et al. 2004 and the Kaggle Grandmasters Playbook](https://claudskills.com/skills/kaggle-hill-climbing/SKILL.md)
- A recommended later refinement: after greedy selection, fine-tune the weights with Nelder-Mead (scipy) on the retained subset only, with weights normalized in absolute value — and switch to Optuna (TPE, 500 trials) with more than 5-8 models, since Nelder-Mead becomes slow — [SKILL.md claudskills.com](https://claudskills.com/skills/kaggle-hill-climbing/SKILL.md)
- Documented pitfalls for weight optimization: a weight > 0.8 on a single model means you are not really blending (add more diversity); optimizing weights on the full OOF without a separate holdout leads to slight overfitting to OOF noise; ignoring correlation between models kills the ensemble benefit; doing weight optimization before greedy selection traps you in a local optimum in high dimension — [SKILL.md claudskills.com](https://claudskills.com/skills/kaggle-hill-climbing/SKILL.md)
- For a custom composite metric (e.g. 0.4×AUC + 0.6×(1−logloss/const)), no source was found documenting this exact practice, but generic tools (nyaggle `averaging_opt`, explicitly described as compatible with an arbitrary evaluation function and a `higher_is_better` flag) in principle allow passing any metric, including a composite one, to an SLSQP weight optimization — [nyaggle docs](https://nyaggle.readthedocs.io/en/stable/source/reference/ensemble.html)

### Inferences
- The existing pipeline (LightGBM + additive CatBoost + P-spline GAM, weighted blend on the probit scale, 10×7 bagging) sits at the "Level 1 + simple weighted blend" level of this hierarchy. The documented gain that remains accessible and not yet exploited would be adding an explicit Level 2 trained on the OOFs (rather than a fixed weighted blend), or replacing the simple weighted blend with greedy selection (hill climbing) on the probit scale, which showed gains of around +0.003 in one documented case (Fertilizer: 0.376 → 0.379) — an order of magnitude comparable to the gap sought (0.741727 → 0.744434).
- The main risk flagged by the literature (weight > 0.8 on one model, optimizing without a holdout separate from the OOF) is relevant here because the number of base models combined (LightGBM, CatBoost, GAM) is small (3), so greedy selection or hill climbing has little room to add real diversity — the main gain would instead come from intra-family diversity (seeds, different additive tree depths) already exploited by the bagging.
- Directly optimizing the blend weights on the exact composite metric of the competition (instead of optimizing AUC and logloss separately then a naive blend) is a defensible and low-regulatory-risk avenue (no external data), but not confirmed by a specific write-up — to be tested internally with care (separate holdout to avoid the overfitting flagged above).

- No write-up documents level-2 stacking applied specifically to a weighted composite AUC/logloss metric (0.4/0.6) — the inference above is not empirically confirmed by a source.
- No figure was found on the precise net gain of 2-level stacking vs a simple weighted blend for a case with only 2-3 model families (most examples found use 75-150 base models, very different from the scale of the user's pipeline).

### Cited Findings

- An educational review of "leaderboard probing" presents it as a technique tied to competition-specific data leakage: some exploits make it possible to recover all public leaderboard labels, others only the label distribution — [mlcourse.ai tutorial](https://nbviewer.jupyter.org/github/Dene33/mlcourse.ai/blob/master/jupyter_english/tutorials/kaggle_leaderboard_probing_nikolai_timonin.ipynb)

- The Tabular Playground Series (Kaggle, launched in 2021, prior to 2025) used synthetic data generated by a deep generative network (CTGAN) from real data — historical information, not 2025-2026, but it confirms that the "reused generator" principle has been a known and documented practice on the Kaggle side for several years — [Packt, The Kaggle Book](https://packt-plus-non-live.prod.packtpub.com/en-at/product/the-kaggle-book-9781801817479/chapter/modeling-for-tabular-competitions-9/section/the-tabular-playground-series-ch09lvl1sec47)

## Which calibration / direct optimization techniques for composite metrics (logloss + AUC) are recommended beyond Platt/isotonic?

### Takeaway
No 2025-2026 source documents a calibration method specifically designed for a weighted composite AUC+logloss metric. Recent academic literature (2025) proposes calibration methods finer than Platt/isotonic (e.g. "clustered calibration" via learned subpopulations), but without an established link to competitions of this kind; the documented competition practice remains: optimize ranking (AUC) via the model, then calibrate separately for the logloss component, evaluating the trade-off on the OOFs.

### Cited Findings
- A recent paper (October 2025, arXiv) titled "Clustered Calibration: Representation-Aware Probability Calibration via Learned Subpopulations" (Lavi, Shapira, Rappoport) proposes calibration per learned subpopulation rather than global calibration (Platt/isotonic) — the full text could not be extracted automatically (PDF not decodable by the fetch tool); only the metadata and the list of sections (Introduction, Benchmarks and Related Work, Calibration Methods, Method, theoretical appendix) could be confirmed; the references cite binary classification tabular datasets (Adult, diabetes 130-US hospitals, Default of Credit Card Clients), suggesting likely applicability to the same type of problem, but this remains an inference from the citations, not a confirmed result — [arXiv 2510.19328](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.19328)
- An older but foundational paper on "field-aware calibration" recalls that AUC is threshold-independent and insensitive to the scale of predictions, which justifies the pattern "optimize ranking with AUC, then correct calibration separately (Platt/isotonic) to improve logloss without changing the ranking" — [arXiv 1905.10713](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.10713)
- Generic blend-weight optimization libraries (nyaggle `averaging_opt`, Endgame `OptimizedBlender`) accept an arbitrary metric function (not only AUC or logloss alone) and a direction flag (maximize/minimize), which in principle makes it possible to pass the competition's composite formula directly as the blend-weight optimization objective rather than optimizing AUC and logloss separately — [nyaggle docs](https://nyaggle.readthedocs.io/en/stable/source/reference/ensemble.html) ; [Endgame docs](https://endgame-ml.readthedocs.io/en/latest/guides/ensembles.html)

## Are there noise injection / label smoothing / targeted perturbation techniques known to help when the label noise is confirmed Gaussian?

### Takeaway
No 2025-2026 source documents a noise injection or label smoothing technique applied to a tabular competition with confirmed Gaussian label noise. The available academic literature shows that label smoothing is mathematically equivalent to symmetric noise injection on the labels and can rival loss-correction methods under label noise, but these results come from general research (vision/text), not from recent tabular competitions, and no direct link to the specific case (Gaussian noise rather than symmetric categorical noise) has been established.

### Cited Findings
- Lukasik et al. (ICML 2020, "Does label smoothing mitigate label noise?") show that label smoothing acts as symmetric noise injection on the labels, and that it can match dedicated loss-correction methods under label noise; they also find that smoothing the teacher model specifically helps in distillation from noisy data — [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/does-label-smoothing-mitigate-label-noise/)
- Eisenbürger et al. (2024/2025) adapt label-noise detection/correction methods to boosted tree models (GBDT), with over 99% noise-detection precision on the Adult dataset, but post-correction performance gains vary by dataset — [alphaXiv 2409.08647](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2409.08647)
- An ICML 2025 study injects 0% to 30% label noise into 15 binary OpenML datasets and compares the robustness of TabPFN to 7 classic classifiers (random forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, SVM, kNN, CatBoost, decision trees) — [ICML 2025](https://icml.cc/virtual/2025/47490)
- An older paper (Automunge, 2022) on stochastic perturbation of tabular features notes that label noise is a special case of feature noise, and that intentional label-noise injection can help compensate for inherent label noise already present (citing Chen et al. 2021) — but this mainly concerns feature noise, not Gaussian label noise specifically — [ar5iv, Automunge](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2202.09248)
- Kim et al. (Machine Learning Journal, 2025) study the impact of label noise on synthetic tabular data generation: quality and utility degrade with increasing noise level, with no significant effect on generator convergence — relevant for understanding the underlying synthetic generator, but not a competition tactic — [mlanthology.org](https://mlanthology.org/mlj/2025/kim2025mlj-empirical)

### Inferences
- If the label noise is confirmed Gaussian (continuous) rather than categorical/symmetric, the results of Lukasik et al. (label smoothing = symmetric noise on categorical labels) do not apply directly: Gaussian noise on a binary target rather suggests that the observed label is a noisy version of a continuous latent probability — in that case, the most defensible avenue (but not confirmed by a competition source) would be to treat the problem as a regression on the latent probability rather than as hard-label classification, which overlaps with the regularization via soft/pseudo-labels already documented in Q1. This remains an inference by the agent, not a cited result.
- No evidence was found that targeted Gaussian noise injection on training labels (in the reverse direction, to "match" the generator's noise) has been tested or documented as a competition tactic — this is ground not covered by the literature found.

### Gaps
- No competition write-up (Kaggle or Zindi, 2025-2026) explicitly mentioning a noise injection or label smoothing technique for a case of confirmed Gaussian label noise was found.
- The link between the literature found (all generic, mostly pre-2025, or academic without a competition anchor) and the user's specific case (confirmed Gaussian noise on Zindi synthetic data) remains speculative — to be treated with caution, no empirical confirmation available.
