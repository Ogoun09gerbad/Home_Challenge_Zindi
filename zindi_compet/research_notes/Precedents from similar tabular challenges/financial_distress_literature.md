# Academic literature: financial distress / liquidity stress / credit scoring from aggregated monthly panels

## Which classic financial indicators/ratios from the liquidity-risk or credit-scoring literature are probably not captured by generic ratios such as "outflow/inflow" or "recent vs older momentum"?

### Takeaway
The academic literature identified is thin and not very quantitative on this specific point; the indicators most often cited by banking practice (DSCR, cash-flow coverage indices, discounted creditworthiness ratio) mainly add a **threshold/debt-structure** dimension (debt service vs EBITDA, discounting of contractual flows) that simple outflow/inflow ratios do not capture — but no study found quantifies the performance gain (AUC) specifically provided by these indicators compared to generic ratios. Notably, the industry leaders in "cash-flow underwriting" (Ocrolus, Plaid, Carrington Labs) themselves build their scores with generic gradient boosting (XGBoost) on hundreds of cash-flow features rather than with domain-specific architectures, which weakens the premise that there is a "secret" financial-domain technique not available in standard ML tooling.

### Cited Findings
- The **Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)** — EBITDA / (principal + interest due over the period) — is described as one of the most critical default-prediction measures used by lenders; a Pepperdine study (2020, cited by a vendor) reports that 85% of institutional lenders consider it important or very important in their credit decision, with a usual threshold ≥1.2 — [Abrigo](https://www.abrigo.com/?p=4977)
- Stevanović (2018, Serbian banking review) reports, via a survey of credit analysts, that **cash-flow-based liquidity indicators, coverage indicators, and cash-flow margin** are perceived as the most used in corporate credit rating systems — [scindeks](https://scindeks-clanci.ceon.rs/data/pdf/1451-4354/2018/1451-43541803012S.pdf)
- An article in the *Journal of the Operational Research Society* (2010) proposes a **creditworthiness index for retail credit** defined as the ratio of the net present value of cash flows actually received to the contractual cash flows — a time-discounting transformation, different from a simple ratio of raw flows — [iaorifors](https://www.iaorifors.com/paper/66774)
- A USPTO patent (8706615) describes a repayment-risk classification method based on **trends in daily inflows/outflows/net flows** (rather than point-in-time levels or ratios) — [USPTO](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8706615)
- Mikulić & Škobić (ECMLG 2025, conference proceedings) study **cash-flow indicators for predicting the credit risk of non-financial companies** using logistic regression on a sample of large companies over 5 years; they conclude that several cash-flow indicators have explanatory value for changes in credit risk, but are **insufficient on their own** for prediction and must be combined with other financial and non-financial measures — [papers.academic-conferences.org](https://papers.academic-conferences.org/index.php/ecmlg/article/download/4263/3894/15532) (the details of the ratios tested could not be extracted; the retrieved PDF was unreadable in text mode)
- In the "cash-flow underwriting" industry: **Ocrolus** uses a gradient boosting model on more than 500 cash-flow features — [Ocrolus](https://docs.ocrolus.com/docs/risk_score); **Plaid LendScore** is built on **XGBoost with monotonicity constraints** (higher balances and more regular inflows → lower predicted risk) — [Plaid](https://plaid.com/blog/how-we-built-lendscore/); **Carrington Labs Cashflow Score 2.0** organizes its score around 5 behavioral categories including Liquidity, Stability and Resilience — [Carrington Labs](https://norfolkdailynews.com/online_features/press_releases/carrington-labs-launches-cashflow-score-2-0-with-expanded-explainability-for-cash-flow-underwriting/article_e0b4ffe5-37c7-51f0-92de-1572dbe8c15a.html)
- On the Zindi "African Credit Scoring Challenge" (platform and task type very close to the current challenge), the decisive feature reported by a participant is a **simple business ratio** (repayment_ratio = Total_Amount_to_Repay / Total_Amount), whose correlation with the target went from near zero to 0.64 once constructed; no scorecard, survival model or domain-specific technique is mentioned in the winning solutions, which rely on XGBoost/CatBoost/LightGBM/MLP stacking — [Zindi discussion](https://zindi.world/competitions/african-credit-scoring-challenge/discussions/24482)
- The **RFM/RFE/RFMTC** frameworks (recency-frequency-monetary, and variants substituting engagement or adding time/churn) are documented as adaptable to mobile money data (inflows/outflows, airtime usage, bill payments), but no academic study explicitly applying RFM to a mobile money credit score was found in this search — [IBM RFM](https://www.ibm.com/support/knowledgecenter/SSLVMB_23.0.0/spss/rfm/rfm_intro.xml.html)

### Inferences
- The documented "domain-specific" indicators (DSCR, coverage ratios, discounted indices) mainly add a dimension **relative to an explicit debt commitment** (contractual payments, repayment schedule) — absent in a task like ours where there is no outstanding loan, only account/mobile money flows. Their transferability to `liquidity_stress_next_30d` is therefore limited without restructuring the problem.
- The fact that the best commercial cash-flow scoring products (Plaid, Ocrolus) themselves use generic gradient boosting with monotonicity constraints — exactly the approach already adopted by the team — suggests that the 0.0025-point composite score gap with rank 1 probably does not come from a missing non-generic domain technique, but more likely from execution differences (tuning, ensembling, noise/quota handling) rather than from a "secret" financial indicator.

### Gaps
- The detailed content (tables of ratios tested, coefficients, significance) of Mikulić & Škobić could not be extracted (binary PDF not readable by the fetch tool) — to revisit if possible via a direct PDF reader.
- No study found explicitly tests whether a "DSCR adapted without formal debt" type indicator (e.g. mandatory expenses / net monthly income ratio) provides a measurable AUC gain over generic flow ratios on mobile money data.
- No source found documents an Altman Z-score adapted to individuals/mobile money with empirical validation.

## Are there published benchmarks (typical AUC/logloss) on similar tasks of predicting financial stress from aggregated monthly/panel data, to compare our current AUC (~0.923) to the literature standard?

### Takeaway
No benchmark found matches the task precisely (6-point monthly panel, mobile money aggregates, 30-day liquidity stress, 15% quota); published AUCs on neighboring credit scoring/default tasks typically range between 0.69 and 0.85, clearly **below** the 0.923 already achieved by the team — which suggests that either the current task is intrinsically more separable (additive generative process + Gaussian noise, known), or these benchmarks are not comparable (different tasks/datasets, different horizons). A scaling-law study on tabular data finds an irreducible noise "floor" for gradient boosting corresponding to an AUROC ≈0.79 in generalization — well below our 0.923, which reinforces the idea that our task is structurally easier/more separable than "real" credit scoring benchmarks (where human behavioral noise is greater).

### Cited Findings
- **PSD2 Explainable AI (arXiv 2011.10367)**: gradient boosting is the best model tested for credit scoring, logistic regression the worst in AUC — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.10367)
- **Kraus & Küchenhoff (LMU Munich thesis)**: on German retail credit data, boosting and an AUC-optimized model outperform standard logistic regression — [edoc.ub.uni-muenchen.de](https://edoc.ub.uni-muenchen.de/17143/1/Kraus_Anne.pdf)
- **Research Square preprint**: mean test AUROC ≈0.788 (95% CI [0.7834; 0.7924]) for gradient boosting ensembles versus 0.767 for logistic regression, a statistically significant difference (p<0.001, Cohen's d = 3.32) — not peer-reviewed — [Research Square](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-10794000)
- **HELOC dataset (arXiv 2009.13384)**: complex ML models only slightly outperform a reference scorecard; the best test results come from a logistic regression with splines — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.13384)
- **2023 credit card dataset**: LightGBM AUC 0.84, MLP 0.83 (logistic baseline not specified in the excerpt) — [arXiv 2310.02956](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.02956)
- **Uganda study (mobile money + Fintech + traditional banking, 2026)**: Random Forest AUC 0.85 (accuracy 96.66%, recall 79.65%), XGBoost AUC 0.81 — [nru.uncst.go.ug](https://nru.uncst.go.ug/items/3581326e-7cc3-42e8-b4a9-452eacfb2b4e)
- **FairLend-Africa (preprint, synthetic data)**: XGBoost AUC 0.7137 on 10,000 synthetic borrowers; logistic regression equivalent — [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/20553705)
- **M-Pesa mobile money adoption/spending (arXiv 1812.03289)**: AUC 0.691 for predicting adoption, 0.619 for spending — a different task (adoption, not default/stress) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.03289)
- **Scaling-law benchmark on tabular data (arXiv, 2026)**: median irreducible noise floor for boosting on full-data classification ≈0.208 (i.e. AUROC ≈0.79) — [arXiv 2607.21866](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.21866)
- An exam example (non-empirical) cites 0.72 (logistic regression) versus 0.81 (gradient boosting) on an SME portfolio — an illustrative value, not empirical evidence.

### Inferences
- Published AUCs in credit scoring/default (0.69–0.85) are consistently well below the 0.923 already obtained on this challenge. This is consistent with the diagnosis already established by the team (generative process = additive score + Gaussian noise, quota-based selection), which makes the task considerably more "learnable" than real credit default (where future human behavior introduces much greater noise than that of a summarized transaction history).
- The irreducible noise floor (~0.79 AUROC) found for boosting on general-purpose tabular data is therefore probably not relevant here: the current task, being more a problem of reconstructing a noisy deterministic signal (additive score + known Gaussian noise) than of predicting human behavior, has a much higher theoretical ceiling — consistent with the fact that the team already reaches 0.923 and that the remaining gap with rank 1 (0.744 vs 0.7419 in composite score) is probably due to approaching the theoretical ceiling (irreducible Gaussian noise of the generator) rather than to a feature engineering deficit.

### Gaps
- No benchmark found specifically covers a **monthly panel with 6 aggregated time points per customer** with a "30-day liquidity stress" target — the credit scoring literature deals almost exclusively with default on an existing loan (horizon often 12-24 months) or service adoption, not with short-term liquidity stress without a formal credit relationship.
- No comparable logloss figure was found in these sources (only AUCs are reported).
- No benchmark found on Zindi/Kaggle for a strictly identical task (the "African Credit Scoring Challenge" is the closest by platform and nature of features, but publishes no AUC in the discussions consulted, only a feature correlation of 0.64).

## Have domain-specific modeling techniques (scorecards, survival models, change-point detection) demonstrated a measurable advantage over generic gradient boosting on this type of task?

### Takeaway
No study found provides a direct, quantified comparison (same AUC, same dataset) between a survival model, a scorecard, or a change-point detection method, and generic gradient boosting on this type of task — this is a clear gap in the accessible literature. The available indirect evidence (logistic scorecard with splines on HELOC; industrial use of XGBoost for cash-flow underwriting) rather suggests that the performance gap between "domain" techniques and well-executed generic ML is **small or zero** once features are well constructed, which runs counter to the hypothesis tested by this research.

### Cited Findings
- **Recurrent-event survival models (Ugur & Zeynalov, working paper 2026)**: on a panel of 28,847 listed companies (1993–2022), modeling repeated distress episodes (rather than a single event) improves prediction; adding a firm-level frailty improves prediction; a parsimonious specification grounded in agency theory outperforms more complex ad hoc models — but **no quantified comparison with gradient boosting** is reported in the excerpt consulted — [fir.vse.cz](https://fir.vse.cz/research/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/Working-paper-2026-01.pdf)
- **Aalen hazard model with MIDAS terms mixing monthly (macro) and quarterly (financial) frequencies** on Chinese companies classified as "ST" (2001–2021): several liquidity and profitability ratios significantly influence the time-varying distress intensity — no quantified comparison with generic ML found — [ideas.repec.org](https://ideas.repec.org/a/mes/emfitr/v60y2024i4p678-687.html)
- **Cox proportional hazards model (Ongoren thesis, 2016)** applied to an unbalanced panel of about 15,600 bank customers, using only basic banking data — no comparative performance figure found in the excerpt — [gcris.khas.edu.tr](https://gcris.khas.edu.tr/entities/publication/c8ec3cb3-79b8-425d-8b8a-c171d07cdf80)
- **Logistic scorecard vs ML (HELOC, arXiv 2009.13384)**: "the more complex ML models showed only slightly better performance than the reference scorecard. The best results... were obtained with a logistic regression using spline transformations" — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.13384)
- **Structural change-point detection**: the literature found (real-time financial monitoring, quickest change-point detection, most-recent-break panels) deals with detecting market/macro crises (bubbles, capital flow reversals), not cash flow or default at the individual borrower level; no study explicitly linking change-point detection to a gain in default/distress prediction was found — [arXiv 2404.03319](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.03319), [arXiv 1509.01570](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.01570), [arXiv 1609.06805](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1609.06805)
- **Dominant industrial use of gradient boosting for cash-flow underwriting** (Ocrolus, Plaid — see question 1): none of these commercial products specialized in financial scoring uses a scorecard, survival model or change-point detection as its main engine; they use generic gradient boosting/XGBoost, sometimes with monotonicity constraints — already the team's practice — [Plaid](https://plaid.com/blog/how-we-built-lendscore/), [Ocrolus](https://docs.ocrolus.com/docs/risk_score)

### Inferences
- The absence of any head-to-head quantified comparison (scorecard/survival/change-point vs GBM) on the same dataset, combined with the widespread industrial use of gradient boosting for cash-flow scoring, constitutes an indication (weak, by absence of evidence) that financial-domain-specific techniques **have not demonstrated a measurable and reproducible advantage** over well-executed generic gradient boosting for this type of aggregated tabular data — contrary to the starting hypothesis of this research.
- Survival models appear to provide documented added value mainly when **repetition of episodes over time** or **censoring** are features of the problem (e.g. firms that enter and exit distress several times over 30 years) — a structure absent from the current Zindi challenge (fixed 6-month history window, fixed 30-day horizon, no repeated episodes), which strongly limits their relevance here.

### Gaps
- No study available online directly compares (on the same dataset, with AUC/logloss) a survival model or a scorecard to gradient boosting on a short-term liquidity stress task from monthly aggregates — this is the central gap of this research, to be flagged explicitly to the author of the final report.
- No study found applies structural change-point detection at the individual customer level (rather than at the market/macro level) to improve a default/distress prediction.
- The Ugur & Zeynalov (2026) document and the Chinese MIDAS study were only consulted via their abstracts/search excerpts, not the full text — a complete reading might reveal quantified comparisons not visible in the excerpts.
