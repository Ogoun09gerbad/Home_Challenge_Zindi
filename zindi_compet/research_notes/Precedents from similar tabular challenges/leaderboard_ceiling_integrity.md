# "Honest" performance ceiling and integrity of Kaggle/Zindi leaderboards

## Are there documented cases of two accounts with nearly identical metrics or an optimal score in a single submission, and what explanations were accepted?

### Takeaway
Cases of identical or nearly identical leaderboard scores between different participants are documented on several platforms (Kaggle, AIcrowd, Codalab, DrivenData), but the explanations vary widely from case to case — a metric computation bug, reference ("baseline") submissions made by mistake, statistical coincidence on a small public split, or genuine rule circumvention (multiple accounts, private code sharing). I found no publicly documented case matching exactly the pattern described (AUC identical to the 6th decimal between two accounts, optimal score from the very first submission) on Kaggle or Zindi — the closest matches involve coincidences on coarser metrics or restricted public splits.

### Cited Findings
- On an AIcrowd challenge, several participants obtained exactly the same score (0.751); the cause confirmed by the organizers was the unintentional submission of a reference ("baseline") model, not fraud — the entries concerned were removed. — [AIcrowd discourse](https://discourse.aicrowd.com/t/too-many-teams-get-the-same-0-751-result-data-leakage-or-real-numbers/7555)
- On another AIcrowd challenge, a user first suspected a scoring bug after two different models produced the same 0.751. — [AIcrowd discourse](https://discourse.aicrowd.com/t/too-many-teams-get-the-same-0-751-result-data-leakage-or-real-numbers/7555)
- On a Codalab forum (ECCV 2020 ChaLearn), a post reports two accounts with a strictly identical leaderboard score; the organizers replied that they were checking, but noted that without already-submitted code it is "very difficult" to distinguish a coincidence from circumvention through multiple accounts. — [Codalab forum](https://competitions.codalab.org/forums/20880/4034)
- On the DrivenData "Rules and submissions" forum, a participant documents identical scores between teams submitted a few minutes apart (examples dated June 4 and July 6, 2019) and writes: "It's extremely unlikely that 2 different people had the same score in the regression task within half an hour." They also point out two very similar usernames ("Thakshi" and "thakshi2345") suspected of being the same person, in violation of the "one account per participant" rule. The thread contains no organizer response or documented investigation outcome. — [DrivenData community](https://community.drivendata.org/t/rules-and-submissions/4563)
- On AIcrowd (KDD Cup), the organizers explicitly notified two accounts with similar names and nearly identical code, demanding a formal explanation of the similarities — an after-the-fact verification procedure rather than public automated detection. — [AIcrowd discourse "Dual participation detected"](https://discourse.aicrowd.com/t/important-dual-participation-detected-please-explain/10726)
- On a Zindi challenge (Makerere Fall Armyworm Crop Challenge), many participants reached an AUC of 1 on a small public split; the organizers confirmed that score ties are broken by submission timestamp order (the earliest wins), and noted that participants can share an identical public score while diverging on the private score — which reduces the significance of a purely public coincidence. — [Zindi discussion](https://zindi.africa/competitions/makerere-fall-armyworm-crop-challenge/discussions/11281)
- The most documented and confirmed case of proven cheating in a major Kaggle competition is that of the "Bestpetting" team (PetFinder.my Adoption Prediction competition, 2019-2020), led by Pavel Pleskov, then a Grandmaster ranked 3rd in the overall Kaggle ranking. The team obtained a score of 0.912, deemed implausible. A third party (Benjamin Micklethoff), involved in productionizing the winning solution for the host, spotted the anomaly and alerted Kaggle. Kaggle confirmed the fraud after investigation: the team allegedly obtained the answers of the private test set (possibly by scraping the host's website), encoded these answers in an ID field of the dataset, and used only part of them to keep a plausible score. Kaggle disqualified the team and banned Pleskov; his employer (H2O.ai) fired him. — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2020/01/21/ai_kaggle_contest_cheat/), [ACM/CACM](https://cacmb4.acm.org/careers/242203-data-science-community-rocked-by-pet-adoption-contest-cheating-scandal)
- One source states that the fraudulent submission was in fact never removed from the publicly displayed leaderboard, which partially contradicts other accounts asserting effective disqualification — an inconsistency between sources to be noted explicitly. — [Medium @u39kun](https://medium.com/feed/@u39kun)
- An academic article (Whitehill, 2017) formally demonstrates that a public log-loss "oracle" (such as a public leaderboard based on log-loss) makes it possible, through a small number of probe submissions, to exactly reconstruct the test set labels without training any classifier. Applied to the Kaggle competition "Intel & MobileODT Cervical Cancer Screening" (stage 1, 512 examples, 3 classes), the attack used fewer than 100 queries (batches of 4 then 6 probed examples) to reconstruct all 512 labels and reach a log-loss of 0.00000 (perfect score), ranking the author 4th out of 848 — the author notes that several other participants were tied on the same perfect score in stage 1. In stage 2 (larger test set), the score dropped to rank 225/848 because the guesses for the remaining 75% of examples were uniform. — [Whitehill, arXiv:1707.01825](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1707.01825)
- The same article concludes that this risk is general for data mining competitions providing precise numerical metric feedback on the test set (not just Kaggle), and recommends that the final evaluation use only examples never seen by the oracle — but it does not quantify the vulnerability of other specific competitions. — [Whitehill, arXiv:1707.01825](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1707.01825)
- A Kaggle product discussion mentions how easy it is to "probe" the public leaderboard when it relies on a large share (25%+ mentioned) of the test set, and proposes a public leaderboard updated progressively instead of an exact instant computation — but the full content of the thread could not be retrieved (the retrieved page contained only the title: "replace public leaderboard with update over time"). — [Kaggle product feedback #297962](https://kaggle.com/product-feedback/297962)

### Inferences
- Confirmed cases of identical score coincidences between different accounts have, in the precedents found, almost always had a mundane cause (bug, baseline submission, shared restricted public split) OR a fraud actively demonstrated after investigation — but never a statistical coincidence accepted as the definitive explanation without verification. No organizer, in the sources found, publicly concluded "it's a coincidence" without prior investigation.
- The specific pattern described by the user (AUC identical to 6 decimals + optimal score in a single submission) is structurally closer to the "leaderboard probing" case (Whitehill) than to the "classic multiple accounts" case: if the synthetic generator of the Zindi challenge has a known structure (additive score + Gaussian noise + exact 15% quota), it is in theory easier to approach the analytical ceiling performance by reverse engineering the mechanism than through a raw leak of the test set — but this remains a hypothesis not confirmed by an external source, to be checked against the details of this challenge's public/private composite metric.
- The fact that no source explicitly documents an "optimal from the very first submission" score on Kaggle or Zindi (apart from the Whitehill case, which is a deliberate academic demonstration, not a real case detected after the fact) suggests either that this pattern is rare, or that it is rarely detected/made public when it occurs in a real prize competition.

### Gaps
- No source found documents a real case (outside academic demonstration) of an "optimal score in a single submission" that was investigated and publicly confirmed or refuted by a Kaggle or Zindi organizer.
- The full content of the Kaggle product-feedback thread on "leaderboard probing" (only the title could be retrieved) could not be analyzed in detail.
- No source specifically addresses a coincidence to 6 decimal places on an AUC/logloss-type metric — the literature found deals with coincidences at 2-3 decimals or with exact scores of 1.0/0.751 on restricted public samples.

## Have Zindi or Kaggle published policies or known incidents regarding prediction sharing/multiple accounts, and how are they detected/sanctioned?

### Takeaway
Zindi has an explicit, public written policy (sections 2 and 7 of its rules) prohibiting multiple accounts and private code sharing, with a two-tier sanctions scale (6-month prize suspension and point deduction for a first offense, permanent ban for a second), and has publicly confirmed having already applied these sanctions. Kaggle does not have an anti-multiple-accounts policy as explicitly documented in the sources found, but has a confirmed history of disqualifications for cheating (the PetFinder.my/Bestpetting case being the most documented), generally detected through reports from other participants rather than through a publicly disclosed automated mechanism.

### Cited Findings
- Official Zindi rules (zindi.world/rules, section 2.2): "Multiple accounts per user are not permitted, and neither is collaboration or membership across multiple teams"; "Individuals and their submissions originating from multiple accounts will be immediately disqualified from the platform." — [Zindi Rules](https://zindi.world/rules)
- Official Zindi rules (section 2.3): "Code must not be shared privately outside of a team"; any shared code must be made accessible to all participants via the platform. The page does not explicitly address the sharing of prediction/submission files (only code). — [Zindi Rules](https://zindi.world/rules)
- Zindi sanctions scale (sections 7.1-7.2): first offense = no prizes for 6 months + deduction of 2000 points from the profile (reduced to 0 if the balance is lower); second offense = account permanently deactivated, with a ban on winning prizes or points through another account. — [Zindi Rules](https://zindi.world/rules)
- Team rules (sections 7.3-7.4): a team with an offending member is not eligible for prizes/points for that competition, regardless of the other members' knowledge; a team with a member on probation (previous offense) cannot win prizes during the 6-month probation period. — [Zindi Rules](https://zindi.world/rules)
- Mandatory code review for the top 10 at close: 48h deadline to provide code or face disqualification (section 4.4); outside the top 10, a 24h deadline or face a 6-month suspension (section 8.4), with account deactivation for repeat offenses. — [Zindi Rules](https://zindi.world/rules)
- Discretionary power clause (sections 9.1-9.2): Zindi may remove or disqualify individuals, teams or accounts "irrespective of your position on the leaderboard". — [Zindi Rules](https://zindi.world/rules)
- A Zindi community update (dated May 20, 2026 according to the search results) confirms that Zindi took enforcement action against several accounts after investigating several competitions, for "prohibited coordinated activity and submission sharing", and thanks the members who reported suspicious activity, specifying that these reports feed into detection and rule enforcement. Zindi states that it cannot comment publicly on individual accounts or investigations. — [Zindi community discussion #1308](https://zindi.world/discussions/1308)
- An older Zindi discussion reports that, as of September 2019 (date of the discussion not specified but earlier), Zindi had disqualified 36 individuals from competitions and banned 4 others from the site. — [Zindi discussion (via search)](https://zindi.africa/discussions/1308)
- Kaggle: the most documented confirmed case of disqualification for cheating is that of the Bestpetting team/Pavel Pleskov (PetFinder.my, 2019-2020), detected through a third-party report to Kaggle rather than through a publicly disclosed automated system; Kaggle conducted an internal investigation and confirmed the fraud before disqualifying the team and banning Pleskov's account. — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2020/01/21/ai_kaggle_contest_cheat/)
- Another highly publicized Kaggle case (Deepfake Detection Challenge, 2020, organized with Facebook) saw the apparent winners disqualified for reasons related to the rules on external data and documentation, which sparked public controversy over the merits of the disqualification — this case illustrates that even legitimate disqualifications can be publicly contested, and does not specifically concern multiple accounts. — [SyncedReview](https://syncedreview.com/2020/06/14/facebook-and-kaggle-face-backlash-after-disqualifying-apparent-deepfake-detection-challenge-winner/)
- On AIcrowd (a different but comparable platform), the organizers explicitly state that using several accounts to circumvent a submission limit is considered "malicious activity" leading to disqualification, while acknowledging that it is "very difficult" to detect private information exchange between participants. — [AIcrowd discourse](https://discourse.aicrowd.com/t/is-teams-allowed)

### Inferences
- Zindi documents and applies its policy more transparently than what was found for Kaggle in this research: Zindi has published a quantified count of sanctions (36 disqualifications + 4 bans as of a given date) and a community communication confirming recent actions against submission sharing — which suggests that sharing predictions between accounts is a recurring and actively monitored problem on the platform, not a theoretical hypothesis.
- Detection, on both platforms, relies largely on reports from other participants (Kaggle PetFinder.my case, Zindi case mentioning "members who reported suspicious activity") rather than on a fully transparent automated system — which indirectly confirms that a reasoned, factual report from a participant can trigger an investigation.

### Gaps
- No official, explicit Kaggle policy on "multiple accounts" could be located in this research (unlike Zindi); a generic Kaggle rules page probably exists, but its precise content was not retrieved.
- The exact technical detection method (IP/device fingerprinting, code similarity analysis, etc.) used by Zindi or Kaggle is not publicly documented in the sources found — a Zindi thread from May 2026 mentions a community proposal for KYC verification and IP/device clustering, but this is only a participant suggestion, not a confirmed policy.
- It is impossible to confirm whether Zindi rule 2.3 (code sharing) explicitly covers the sharing of raw prediction files (.csv submissions) separately from source code sharing — the retrieved page mentions only "code".

## Is there a published statistical analysis of the probability that an AUC coincidence to 6 decimals occurs by chance between two independent participants (competition with about 30000 test rows)?

### Takeaway
No published statistical analysis specifically addressing the probability of an AUC coincidence to 6 decimals between two independent participants was found. The available literature provides adjacent tools (exact distribution of the AUC for a random classifier, variance of ranking metrics across splits) but no direct computation applicable to the specific case described.

### Cited Findings
- An academic article (Zeinstra et al.) gives the exact probability distribution of the AUC for a purely random classifier, given a finite number of distinct scores, via a restricted partition function — this is the closest tool found for analytically computing the probability of a given AUC value, but it does not address the case of two different (non-random) classifiers converging to the same value to 6 decimals. — [Research Utwente](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/how-random-is-a-classifier-given-its-area-under-curve/)
- An article (arXiv:1709.02418) studies how knowledge of the AUC constrains the set of possible labelings of the dataset — conceptually relevant but provides no computation of the probability of a coincidence between two participants. — [arXiv:1709.02418](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.02418)
- A blog (Andrew Wheeler) shows, through repeated resampling, that the relative ranking of two models on a train/test split can flip depending on the random draw of the split — this illustrates the variance of ranking metrics but does not quantify the probability of an exact tie to 6 decimals. — [Andrew Wheeler blog](https://andrewpwheeler.com/page/19/?cat=-1)
- On a Zindi challenge (Makerere Fall Armyworm), many participants reached an AUC of 1.0 (a trivially likely tie because it is bounded and based on a very small public split), with the organizers breaking ties by submission timestamp order — this case illustrates that an exact metric tie is statistically more likely on a restricted, noisy public split than on a large-scale composite score, but provides no computation generalizable to the 30000-row case mentioned by the user. — [Zindi discussion](https://zindi.africa/competitions/makerere-fall-armyworm-crop-challenge/discussions/11281)

### Inferences
- In the absence of a specific published analysis, any estimate of the probability of a 6-decimal coincidence on ~30000 test rows should be produced by internal simulation (resampling under the null hypothesis), not cited as an established fact from the external literature — no source found allows asserting a reliable order of magnitude for this specific case.

### Gaps
- No publication (article, blog post, forum discussion) explicitly and quantitatively addressing the probability of an AUC/logloss coincidence to 6 decimals between two independent accounts was located. This is a clear gap in the available public literature, not just in this research: the topic seems too specific/niche to have been the subject of a dedicated published analysis.
- No published analysis was found on the typical gap between the best "honest" score achievable on a challenge with a known synthetic generator and the score displayed at the top of the leaderboard — the academic literature found on Bayes error/irreducible error (see below) is general and does not concern specific Kaggle/Zindi competitions with synthetic data.
- Indirect note: the search on "Bayes error/irreducible error in synthetic benchmarks" only returned general academic work on Bayes error estimation (e.g. NeurIPS 2021 on normalizing flow models, a 2025 thesis on selective prediction) with no direct link to public data science competitions — to be treated as not directly relevant rather than as confirming or refuting the empirical ceiling of 0.2283 computed by the user.

## What is the recommended practice for a participant who suspects an irregularity at the top of the leaderboard?

### Takeaway
The documented precedents converge on a two-step practice: report factually to the organizers/platform (without unsubstantiated public accusations), then keep focusing on one's own performance and on compliance with the rules — the platforms (Zindi in particular) state explicitly that they do not comment publicly on individual investigations, and the confirmed disqualification cases on Kaggle originated from private reports to the platform, not from open public discussions.

### Cited Findings
- In the Kaggle PetFinder.my case, the detection of the fraud originated from a private report by a third-party participant directly to Kaggle (not from a public accusation on the forum), followed by an internal investigation by the platform before any announcement. — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2020/01/21/ai_kaggle_contest_cheat/)
- Zindi explicitly states that it cannot comment publicly on individual accounts or investigations, while confirming that member reports contribute to rule detection and enforcement — which suggests that the appropriate route is a private channel (support/organizers) rather than a public discussion on the competition forum. — [Zindi community discussion #1308](https://zindi.world/discussions/1308)
- On AIcrowd, an organizer faced with a report of very similar accounts chose to contact the accounts concerned directly to obtain a formal explanation rather than act without hearing both sides — illustrating an organizer practice of verification before sanction. — [AIcrowd "Dual participation detected"](https://discourse.aicrowd.com/t/important-dual-participation-detected-please-explain/10726)
- Conversely, on the public DrivenData forum, a participant publicly named suspect teams and accounts by rank and username without any visible organizer response in the thread — this case illustrates a counter-example where direct public accusation did not (in the available traces) lead to a visible resolution, which argues rather for the private route. — [DrivenData community](https://community.drivendata.org/t/rules-and-submissions/4563)
- Zindi rules (sections 9.1-9.2) confirm that the platform retains discretionary power to remove/disqualify accounts "irrespective of your position on the leaderboard", which means that a reasoned report can be processed even after the competition closes or after a rank has apparently been awarded. — [Zindi Rules](https://zindi.world/rules)

### Inferences
- The pattern emerging from the precedents (PetFinder.my, AIcrowd dual-participation) is: gather the observable factual elements (e.g. public score identical to N decimals, number of submissions, dates), send them to the platform's support/organizer channel rather than to the public discussion forum, and let the investigation (with access to internal data: IP, submitted code, history) proceed without a named public accusation — this approach is consistent with the user's constraints (stay factual, do not accuse publicly) and with Zindi's investigation confidentiality clause.
- Continuing to compete normally in parallel (not abandoning one's own strategy on the basis of an unconfirmed suspicion) is implicitly the default practice in all the cases found: in none of the precedents did a participant see their own ranking improve before the platform actually resolved the investigation.

### Gaps
- No source documents a written Zindi or Kaggle policy formally describing "how to report suspected cheating" (dedicated form, support email, processing time) — only the general rules page and an after-the-fact community communication were found, not a procedural guide.
- No information was found on the average processing time of a report or on the likelihood that an unconfirmed report leads to any feedback at all for the reporting person (beyond Zindi's statement that "we cannot comment publicly on individual investigations").
