# No quantified precedent covers this exact gap

Four cross-checked research threads (financial-distress literature, statistical theory of quota+noise, Kaggle/Zindi/DrivenData competition write-ups, and leaderboard integrity) converge on a single, honest conclusion: **no source found documents, for a generating mechanism identical to ours (additive hidden score + independent Gaussian noise + selection of the top 15%), a quantified result that demonstrates or precisely bounds a gap of 0.003 to 0.005 in composite score**. The academic signal-detection literature provides an exact formula linking AUC to the standardized signal separation, but no data make it possible to say whether our residual gap is irreducible noise or a real margin. Write-ups from synthetic competitions (Kaggle Instant Gratification, Tabular Playground Series, Don't Overfit) show statistically related generators (Gaussian mixtures, CTGAN), but none uses our exact-quota mechanism, and none reports a score gain of the same order of magnitude as ours in an equivalent context. The financial-distress literature does, however, confirm that our current AUC (0.923) already far exceeds the usual bounds of real-world credit scoring (0.69–0.85), which points toward a generator noise ceiling rather than a missing technique. On the leaderboard anomaly (AUC identical to the 6th decimal between two accounts with very different submission histories), no exact precedent exists, but two academic cases and one confirmed fraud case provide a cautious interpretive framework without allowing a conclusion. Honesty therefore requires telling the user that the targeted gap is neither confirmed reachable nor confirmed out of reach by any external precedent — the research closes the bibliographic question without settling it empirically.

## Credit-scoring benchmarks plateau well below our AUC

The search of the academic financial-distress and credit-scoring literature found no benchmark specifically covering a monthly panel with 6 aggregated time points and a 30-day liquidity-stress target — the central gap in this corpus. But published AUCs on neighboring tasks (credit default, retail scoring, cash-flow underwriting) consistently fall between **0.69 and 0.85**, well below the 0.923 already achieved on this challenge: the PSD2 Explainable AI study finds gradient boosting better than logistic regression for credit scoring ([arXiv 2011.10367](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.10367)), a Research Square preprint reports a mean AUROC of 0.788 for boosting ensembles versus 0.767 for logistic regression ([Research Square](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-10794000)), the HELOC dataset sees complex ML models only slightly outperform a reference scorecard ([arXiv 2009.13384](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.13384)), and a Ugandan study on mobile money + fintech + traditional banking reports an AUC of 0.85 for Random Forest and 0.81 for XGBoost ([nru.uncst.go.ug](https://nru.uncst.go.ug/items/3581326e-7cc3-42e8-b4a9-452eacfb2b4e)). A scaling-law benchmark on tabular data even finds a median irreducible-noise floor for boosting corresponding to an AUROC ≈0.79 ([arXiv 2607.21866](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.21866)) — well below our 0.923, which suggests this generic floor does not apply here: our task, being the reconstruction of a deterministic additive signal corrupted by known Gaussian noise rather than the prediction of future human behavior, has a structurally higher theoretical ceiling. Notably, and helpful for ruling out the hypothesis of a missing domain technique: the industry leaders in cash-flow underwriting (Ocrolus, with more than 500 cash-flow features; Plaid LendScore, built on XGBoost with monotonicity constraints) themselves use generic gradient boosting rather than scorecards, survival models, or change-point detection specific to the financial domain ([Ocrolus](https://docs.ocrolus.com/docs/risk_score), [Plaid](https://plaid.com/blog/how-we-built-lendscore/)). No study found compares head-to-head with numbers (same AUC metric, same dataset) a survival model, a scorecard, or change-point detection against generic gradient boosting on this type of task — a clear gap in the literature, but one whose absence, combined with industry practice, weighs rather against the idea that a domain-specific technique would bring a measurable gain on our already highly optimized pipeline.

## Quota-noise theory gives formulas, not a comparable number

The statistical theory of exact-quota selection on an additive score + Gaussian noise (related to Tobit/Heckman/censored probit) provides a closed-form and directly relevant result — but no bound numerically comparable to our gap of 0.003–0.005. The binormal signal-detection model gives the exact formula **AUC = Φ(a/√2)** for equal variances, where `a` is the standardized separation of the signal means ([MetricGate, Parametric ROC](https://metricgate.com/docs/parametric-roc-signal-detection/)), and a generalization to unequal variances **AUC = Φ(a/√(1+b²))** has existed since Metz & Kronman (1980) ([MetricGate, Metz binormal fit](https://metricgate.com/docs/metz-binormal-roc-fit/)). For logloss, the entropy/KL decomposition confirms that the minimum achievable loss, when the predictor is exactly calibrated, is the entropy of the true distribution: E[logloss] = H(D) + KL(D‖q̂) ([entropy/KL source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.00252)). A paper dedicated to estimating the "Bayes-optimal BER and AUC" under noise (Ushio, Ishida, Sugiyama) exists, but its detailed content could not be extracted — neither the PDF nor the HTML version (404) was accessible, leaving a potentially relevant lead unverified. In terms of concrete techniques beyond standard probit, Matias (2002) shows that an **unknown** noise variance seriously degrades the convergence rate of deconvolution compared with a known variance, and that this degradation propagates to nonlinear errors-in-variables models ([Matias 2002](https://www.numdam.org/articles/10.1051/ps:2002015/)) — which concretely implies that better estimating the generator's noise σ (via a Tobit-type EM scheme rather than a plain probit fit) potentially pays off more than merely specifying the correct link. But none of the sources found — neither classical Tobit/Heckman theory nor errors-in-variables theory (attenuation, Spearman's formula) — addresses the specific case of **exact-quota selection** (as opposed to a fixed threshold), where each individual's label depends on the relative ranking of all the others; this is a structural gap in the accessible literature, not just in this search. Consequently, no source makes it possible to say whether our gap of 0.003–0.005 in composite score (0.002–0.005 in logloss) is "within the expected order of magnitude" of residual selection noise or represents a real, achievable margin for improvement; any definitive answer would require an internal Monte Carlo simulation with known σ and quota rather than further bibliographic research.

## Documented synthetic generators do not reproduce our mechanism

No Kaggle, Zindi, or DrivenData competition (2022-2026) found publicly confirms a generator combining additive score + Gaussian noise + exact-quota selection like ours. The best-documented synthetic generators use other mechanisms: Kaggle's Tabular Playground Series (TPS), launched in 2021, used CTGAN networks to replicate public datasets or past competitions ([Kaggle Book, ch.9](https://packt-plus-non-live.prod.packtpub.com/en-at/product/the-kaggle-book-9781801817479/chapter/modeling-for-tabular-competitions-9/section/the-tabular-playground-series-ch09lvl1sec47)), and the "Instant Gratification" competition (2019) relied on multi-cluster Gaussian mixtures per class — a 6th-place write-up reports that using 3 clusters per class (rather than 2) improved validation and test AUC, with most of the gain coming from reproducing the generator's structure rather than from the choice of algorithm. The research identified, with high probability but without external confirmation of the generation mechanism, the likely name of our own competition on Zindi — the **"AI4EAC Liquidity Stress Early Warning Challenge"** — through an exact match of the scoring formula (logloss weighted at 60% + AUC weighted at 40%, i.e. exactly our `0.4*AUC + 0.6*(1-logloss/0.595)`) and of the target definition (30-day liquidity stress, students from East/Central Africa) ([Zindi, competition page](https://zindi.africa/competitions/liquidity-stress-early-warning-challenge)). But the public data page neither confirms nor refutes the score+noise+quota mechanism, leaving this knowledge as an internal inference by the team, neither corroborated nor contradicted by an external source. No competition write-up found reports a precise numerical delta in logloss/AUC/composite score corresponding to the "last 0.5-1%" in a context strictly analogous to ours — this is the gap most directly relevant to the question asked: the competition literature simply does not provide a quantified point of comparison for this exact type of generator.

## The leaderboard anomaly recalls two precedents, without confirming them

On the troubling observation — AUC identical to the 6th decimal between ranks 1 and 3 despite 1 vs 25 submissions, and rank 1's score obtained on its very first submission — no source documents an exactly matching real case, but two bodies of precedent frame a cautious reading. On one side, Whitehill's paper formally demonstrates that a public log-loss oracle makes it possible, with a small number of probe submissions (fewer than 100 in his application to a real Kaggle competition), to reconstruct the test-set labels and reach a perfect score without training any classifier — applied to the "Intel & MobileODT Cervical Cancer Screening" competition, this attack produced a log-loss of 0.00000, and several participants were tied on that perfect score ([Whitehill, arXiv:1707.01825](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1707.01825)). The fact that our competition has a known generating mechanism (score+noise+quota) would, in theory, make reverse-engineering the mechanism easier than a raw leak — but this remains a hypothesis not confirmed by any external source. On the other side, the confirmed and quantified fraud case on Kaggle (PetFinder.my, team "Bestpetting" led by Grandmaster Pavel Pleskov) shows how an anomaly was detected in practice: a third-party participant noticed an abnormally wide score gap between 1st and 2nd place, examined the winning code, and found a lookup of scraped labels hidden in the data — Kaggle confirmed the fraud, disqualified the team (forfeiting the USD 10,000 prize) and banned the account, and Pleskov's employer fired him ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2020/01/21/ai_kaggle_contest_cheat/)). Our case differs on one key point: the signal typically used to detect an anomaly in the precedents (an abnormal gap between rank 1 and rank 2, or a divergence between public and final leaderboard) does not apply directly here, since it is instead an extreme **similarity** (6th decimal) between two different ranks that is puzzling — a pattern none of the sources consulted addresses by name. Zindi has a written and enforced policy against multiple accounts and code sharing (first offense: 6 months without prizes + removal of 2000 points; second offense: permanent ban), and publicly confirmed (May 20, 2026) that it had sanctioned several accounts for "prohibited coordinated activity and submission sharing" in other recent competitions ([Zindi Rules](https://zindi.world/rules), [Zindi community discussion #1308](https://zindi.world/discussions/1308)). No published statistical analysis quantifies the probability that a 6-decimal AUC coincidence occurs by chance between two independent participants on ~30,000 test rows — the closest literature (exact distribution of the AUC for a random classifier, by Zeinstra et al.) does not address the case of two non-random classifiers converging to that precision ([Research Utwente](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/how-random-is-a-classifier-given-its-area-under-curve/)). The recommended practice, which emerges consistently from the precedents (private report to Kaggle in the PetFinder.my case, confidentiality policy for individual investigations at Zindi), is to gather the observable facts (score identical to N decimals, number and dates of submissions) and pass them to the platform's support/organizers rather than to the public forum, without naming anyone, while continuing to compete normally in parallel — in no precedent found did a participant see their ranking improve before an investigation was resolved.

## Conclusion

The honest answer to the question asked has three parts. First, the targeted gap (0.003–0.005 composite score) is **neither confirmed reachable nor confirmed out of reach** by any quantified external precedent: no academic or competition source addresses the exact score+noise+quota mechanism with a comparable numerical result, and the only closed-form formula available (binormal AUC = Φ(a/√2)) allows the calculation but not an empirical verdict without knowing the generator's true signal-to-noise ratio — a limitation acknowledged by the research itself, which recommends an internal Monte Carlo simulation rather than a new literature review. Second, this research surfaces no genuinely new technical lead beyond what has already been tested and judged to bring no measurable gain (Platt, isotonic, winsorizing, exact-quota shift) — the only partially new and speculative lead is explicit estimation of σ (noise variance) via a Tobit-type EM scheme rather than a plain probit maximum likelihood, suggested by the convergence degradation documented by Matias (2002) under unknown variance; its potential gain on our precise gap is guaranteed by no source. Finally, on the leaderboard anomaly, caution is warranted: in decreasing order of plausibility among the explanations structurally compatible with the precedents found, we retain (1) genuine skill and a near-optimal solution submitted right away — a rank 1 obtained in a single submission is documented nowhere as a sign of cheating in itself — (2) exploitation of the generator's known mechanism (score+noise+quota), allowing analytic convergence toward a near ceiling without many iterations, and (3), less likely to be demonstrable without access to the platform's internal data, information or submission sharing between accounts, for which Zindi has an active history of recent sanctions but no public confirmation specific to this competition. If the user wishes to report the anomaly, the practice aligned with all the precedents consulted is a factual, private report to Zindi support (score, decimals, dates and number of submissions), without public accusation, while pursuing their own modeling strategy in parallel.
