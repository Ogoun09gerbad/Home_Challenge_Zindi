# Why ensemble tactics beat new algorithms here

Across four independent research threads — recent boosting/GAM literature, tabular deep learning, the statistical theory of quota-based selection, and competition tactics — the evidence converges on one honest conclusion: **no newly published algorithm (2024-2026) is documented to close a gap this specific and this small**. The target is a logloss of 0.223771 and AUC of 0.925214 against a current 0.741727 public score; the pipeline already encodes the right inductive bias (depth-1 additive trees + P-spline GAM under a probit link for a confirmed additive-signal-plus-Gaussian-noise generator), and the literature on tabular foundation models, rebalanced-loss GBDTs, and neural additive models offers no case tested on a comparable size/imbalance/structure combination that beats such a blend. The statistical literature on exact-quota selection (Cosslett, Imbens, Imbens & Lancaster) confirms that knowing "exactly 15% positive" can sharpen estimation of a **threshold parameter**, not the shape of the additive score itself, and explicitly argues against the one class of estimator (semi-parametric single-index methods) that might otherwise seem attractive. The most actionable, best-evidenced levers are not new models but second-order ensembling and calibration tactics documented in recent Kaggle Grandmaster write-ups — direct optimization of blend weights on the competition's own composite metric, and careful fold-wise pseudo-labeling — which have produced gains of the same order of magnitude (~0.003) as the gap to rank 1. A separate, uncomfortable finding is that the historical "leaderboard-probing" literature raises a real possibility that the rank-1 score is not reachable through modeling at all.

## Boosting's 2024-2026 gains are engineering, not accuracy

A systematic look at what changed in gradient boosting between 2024 and 2026 turns up almost nothing that targets the pipeline's actual regime — an additive, Gaussian-noised, rare-target signal at ~40k rows. The XGBoost v3.0/v3.1 and LightGBM v4.6/v4.7 changelogs are dominated by external-memory training, GPU support, categorical re-encoding, and Arrow/Polars I/O — "no novelty touching modeling precision on an additive signal or rare target" ([XGBoost v3.0 changelog](https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/en/stable/changes/v3.0.0.html), [LightGBM v4.6](https://newreleases.io/project/pypi/lightgbm/release/4.6.0)). The closest thing to a genuine accuracy lever is a family of **rebalanced losses** for GBDTs (weighted cross-entropy, focal loss, ASL, and two new variants ACE/AWE) tested by Luo, Yuan and Xu across 40 imbalanced datasets, improving F1 on 13 of 15 binary tasks by 0.38-28.91% — but the metric reported is F1 at a decision threshold, not logloss or AUC, and none of the 40 datasets matches the competition's combination of ~40k rows and exactly 15% positives ([arXiv 2407.14381](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.14381v1)). The most genuinely "new" 2024-2026 development is tabular foundation models — TabPFN-2.5 and BoostPFN — which claim to beat tuned GBDTs by default, including on wide feature sets, but these are self-reported claims not independently replicated for a confirmed additive, low-dimensional signal ([arXiv 2511.08667](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08667), [arXiv 2503.01256](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.01256)). On the question closest to the pipeline's actual loss function — boosting under a probit or cloglog link rather than logistic — no mainstream library has added native support in 2024-2026; the only directly relevant method found, "Probit Model Tree" boosting with sub-bagging (SBPMT), predates the window (November 2023) and was tested only on a classic simulation, not validated against a custom-probit GBDT on comparable data ([arXiv 2311.02827](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2311.02827)). On GAM refinements specifically, Schulte & Rügamer's 2025 analysis of componentwise P-spline boosting is the one practically relevant finding: it shows the boosted P-spline solution converges toward the **unpenalized** B-spline fit regardless of the penalty, meaning effective regularization is controlled by the number of boosting iterations (early stopping) rather than the penalty parameter chosen a priori — a tuning implication for the GAM component worth checking, though no quantitative comparison against a GBDT+GAM blend was found ([arXiv 2503.05538](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05538)). No source identified any variance-reduction technique (DART-style tree dropout, feature perturbation, model distillation) with a documented net gain at the tens-of-thousands-of-rows scale beyond what Minimal Variance Sampling — already CatBoost's default and the likely basis of the existing 10-fold × 7-seed bagging — already provides ([arXiv 1910.13204](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1910.13204)).

## Deep learning offers no edge on an additive, Gaussian signal

The case for tabular deep learning as a path to the record is weak on three independent, converging grounds. First, the living benchmarks most recent to this window — TabArena (51 curated datasets, 16 model families) and OmniTabBench (3,030 datasets) — both explicitly conclude there is **no universal winner**: "gradient boosted trees remain strong on practical tabular datasets. Deep learning has caught up with larger time budgets, especially through hyperparameter-configuration ensembling," and protocol/post-hoc ensembling choices move rankings more than architecture does ([arXiv 2506.16791](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.16791), [alphaXiv 2604.06814](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.06814v1)). Second, the dataset's ~40,000 rows sit in a genuine gray zone: TabPFN v2 is explicitly recommended only "up to 10,000 samples," and even TabPFN-2.5's "Scaling Mode," tested from 1M to 10M rows against CatBoost/XGBoost/LightGBM, states outright that **"there is no evidence the gap with gradient boosting narrows as scale increases"** — meaning the foundation-model advantage, where it exists, does not extend toward this dataset's size, and ordinary gradient boosting does not lose ground either ([Prior Labs technical report](https://priorlabs.ai/technical-reports/large-data-model), [TabPFN-2 docs](https://priorlabs.ai/tabpfn-2)). Third, and most decisively for this specific problem, the structure of the signal argues against generic deep learning on inductive-bias grounds rather than just benchmark averages: a 2025/2026 systematic review of GAMs versus neural networks across 143 papers and 430 datasets finds GAMs "remain competitive, particularly on small data," with any NN advantage on larger data categories becoming **"non-significant in recent years"** ([arXiv 2510.24601](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.24601v2)). The pipeline's depth-1 trees plus P-spline GAM already impose exactly the additive inductive bias that matches the confirmed data-generating process; a generic neural network (TabPFN, FT-Transformer, SAINT) would have to *learn from data* that there are no interactions, a cost not offset on a ~40k-row, ~6,000-positive sample. Even GAMformer, the one neural architecture built with additivity as an explicit prior, claims only parity with existing GAMs, not superiority over a GBM+GAM blend ([arXiv 2410.04560](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.04560v1)); ProtoNAM's own related-work section concedes neural GAMs "remain behind tree-based GAMs in many tabular applications" ([arXiv 2410.04723](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.04723v1)). Hybrid boosting-neural architectures (NODE, GrowNet) fare no better: both are structurally still additive sums of weak learners, offering no different bias than depth-1 boosted trees, and neither appears among the curated model families in TabArena's 2025 living benchmark — a tacit sign the community no longer considers them competitive ([arXiv 2506.16791](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.16791)). The one defensible, literature-supported use of deep learning here is not as a replacement but as **one additional low-correlation blend component** (a small regularized MLP, or GAMformer), in the same spirit as the existing LightGBM+CatBoost+GAM blend — a Kaggle-practitioner guide notes neural networks "can add signal in ensemble" even where GBDTs dominate tabular competitions outright ([Notion guide, NN for tabular competitions](https://decisive-willow-f4d.notion.site/Neural-networks-for-tabular-competitions-a72d7faa776f4e85ac1978478f120213)), but no source documents this closing a gap of this size on a confirmed additive-plus-Gaussian signal.

## Quota information sharpens the threshold, not the shape of s(x)

The statistical literature on estimation under an exactly-known quota is mature but narrower in scope than it first appears. A well-established economics result — running from Cosslett's 1987 efficiency bounds through Imbens (1992) and Imbens & Lancaster (1996) — shows formally that incorporating a **known aggregate share** (population choice probabilities, or an exact count of cases) as an additional moment constraint yields an estimator strictly more efficient than a standard MLE that lets this share float freely ([Imbens 1992, EMM estimator](https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/imbens/files/an_efficient_method_of_moments_estimator_for_discrete_choice_models_with_choice-based_sampling.pdf); [Imbens & Lancaster 1996](https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/imbens/files/efficient_estimation_and_stratified_sampling.pdf)). Critically, however, in every case located, the documented gain is on a **location/intercept parameter** — the decision threshold — not on the shape of the additive score function s(x) itself; no source derives an estimator that recovers an additive s(x) more efficiently under exact top-K-by-position censoring than a correctly specified probit MLE does. The mathematically closest framework to the competition's actual mechanism (sort by a noisy score, keep the top 15% by position, observe covariates only for the selected rows) is the theory of **concomitants of order statistics** and, in reliability theory, **Type II censoring** — but no paper was found building a regression theory for recovering an additive function under this exact setup, which appears to be a genuine gap in the literature rather than an oversight in the search ([Concomitant statistics, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concomitant_(statistics)); [Nagaraja & David 1994](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/437276)). Two actionable, better-sourced levers follow from the adjacent machine-learning literature on exact marginal constraints: positive-unlabeled (PU) learning, where a known class prior permits valid classifier training and offers three concrete correction families — post-processing, example reweighting, or building the prior directly into the learning objective ([Elkan & Noto-derived synthesis](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1601.01944); [ADAC 2021](https://home.ipipan.waw.pl/j.mielniczuk/ADAC_2021.pdf)) — and rank-preserving calibration under exact known totals, formalized by Sood as a projection onto the intersection of the probability simplex and an isotonic-plus-sum-constraint set, explicitly designed to avoid the scrambled ranking that a naive prior correction via EM/label-shift can cause ([gsood.com](https://gsood.com/research/papers/multiclass-rank-calibration.pdf)). A 2025 theoretical paper on optimal decision rules under a quota cap independently confirms that the optimal classifier under a fixed-fraction cap is a threshold rule on the score — validating, but not improving upon, the pipeline's existing probit-plus-threshold approach ([arXiv 2511.08347](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.08347)). Equally important is a negative result worth taking seriously: semi-parametric single-index estimators (Klein-Spady, Ichimura, Han's maximum rank correlation) are built to relax the assumption on the noise distribution F — but F (Gaussian) has already been empirically confirmed here, so these estimators would pay an efficiency cost for robustness that is not needed. Cosslett (1987) shows adaptive (loss-free) estimation is generally impossible when F is unknown, and Sherman (1993) proves Han's MRC estimator does not even reach the semi-parametric efficiency bound for binary choice ([Cosslett 1987](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1987/05/01/efficiency-bounds-distribution-free-estimators-binary-choice); [Sherman 1993](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/econometric-theory/article/maximum-rank-correlation-estimator-and-the-rank-estimator-in-binary-choice-models/362E4E43A54E35E55A564F26FF1D35A6)). These methods also assume a **linear** index, the opposite of the problem's actual non-linear additive s(x) — a double mismatch that makes this family a clear "don't test" recommendation.

## Blend-weight optimization matches the exact size of gap needed

The strongest, most directly transferable evidence in this entire review comes not from new algorithms but from current Kaggle Grandmaster competition practice, documented in the NVIDIA-hosted "Kaggle Grandmasters Playbook" (Chris Deotte, Kazuki Onodera, Théo Viel, Mrigendra Agrawal). Two concrete numbers matter. First, simple seed-aggregation (100 XGBoost seeds vs. a single seed) stabilized a leaderboard metric from ~0.376 to ~0.379 — a **+0.003 gain purely from aggregation**, the same order of magnitude as the 0.741727 → 0.744434 gap to rank 1 here ([NVIDIA Developer Blog, Playbook](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data)). Second, beyond simple weighted blending, the documented next step is **greedy forward selection with replacement** ("hill climbing," equivalent to Caruana et al. 2004's ensemble selection): start from the best single model, iteratively add candidates (any candidate reusable) only if validation improves, and derive final weights from selection counts — a technique a March 2026 Playground winner pushed to a 4-level, 150-model stack chosen from 850 experiments ([NVIDIA Developer Blog, Playbook](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data); [Grandmaster stacking write-up](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/grandmaster-pro-tip-winning-first-place-in-a-kaggle-competition-with-stacking-using-cuml/)). Documented pitfalls matter equally: a weight above 0.8 on one model signals the blend isn't really diversifying; optimizing weights on the full out-of-fold set without a separate holdout overfits to OOF noise; and optimizing weights before greedy selection traps the search in a local optimum ([hill-climbing SKILL.md, citing Caruana et al. 2004](https://claudskills.com/skills/kaggle-hill-climbing/SKILL.md)). Because generic blend-optimization tools (nyaggle's `averaging_opt`, Endgame's `OptimizedBlender`) accept an arbitrary metric function, the most concrete, low-risk, not-yet-exploited step is to stop optimizing AUC and logloss separately and instead **optimize blend weights directly on the competition's own composite metric** on the probit scale ([nyaggle docs](https://nyaggle.readthedocs.io/en/stable/source/reference/ensemble.html)) — an inference grounded in generic tooling rather than a dedicated write-up, but a safe one given the existing infrastructure. Pseudo-labeling is the other major lever but carries real risk here: the same Playbook recommends soft (probabilistic) labels, fold-wise recomputation of pseudo-labels to avoid validation leakage, and a final fine-tune on original labels only ("the stronger the model, the better the pseudo-labels") ([NVIDIA Developer Blog, Playbook](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/the-kaggle-grandmasters-playbook-7-battle-tested-modeling-techniques-for-tabular-data); [fold-wise leakage caution](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/grandmaster-pro-tip-winning-first-place-in-a-kaggle-competition-with-stacking-using-cuml/)) — but Zindi's own rules are inconsistent across competitions: staff explicitly banned test-set-informed training in one challenge under threat of disqualification, while a moderator in a different challenge called pseudo-labeling "widely used" and permitted ([Zindi SDG discussion](https://zindi.africa/competitions/sustainable-development-goals-sdgs-text-classification-challenge/discussions/38); [Zindi GBV discussion](https://zindi.africa/competitions/gender-based-violence-tweet-classification-challenge/discussions/9122)). Given the confirmed Gaussian label noise, aggressive pseudo-labeling near the decision boundary (p≈0.5) is exactly where that noise dominates, so any implementation needs confidence filtering and must be checked against this specific competition's rules before use.

## The rank-1 gap may not be a modeling problem at all

One finding earns a dedicated, uncomfortable section: no 2025-2026 write-up documents reverse-engineering a synthetic generator's seed or exploiting an exact-quota mechanism in a live competition, but a well-established historical precedent is directly structurally relevant. Whitehill's "Climbing the Kaggle Leaderboard by Exploiting the Log-Loss Oracle" showed that repeated submissions to a public scoreboard can be used to infer batches of test labels without training any classifier at all, reaching 4th place in a 2017 Kaggle competition through oracle exploitation alone, not modeling ([arXiv/ar5iv 1707.01825](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1707.01825)). This is cited here not as a confirmed explanation of any specific leaderboard anomaly, but as the honest statistical backdrop against which a user-observed pattern — two different leaderboard ranks sharing an AUC identical to the sixth decimal despite wildly different submission counts — should be read: it is structurally consistent with score-oracle exploitation or prediction-sharing between accounts, though no source in this search confirms or refutes it for the specific competition in question. If something like this is occurring at the top of the leaderboard, there is no legitimate countermeasure — replicating a leak or shared account would itself be a rules violation — and the only honest response is to accept that rank 1 may be unreachable by modeling means, and to optimize what legitimate modeling can actually move.

## Conclusion

The four-part review converges on a single asymmetry: the literature is rich in theory explaining *why* the current pipeline is already close to optimal for this exact generative mechanism, and comparatively thin on anything proven to beat it. The highest-value next actions are cheap, low-risk, and already compatible with the existing 10-fold × 7-seed infrastructure — not a model rebuild. In priority order: (1) replace the current fixed-weight probit-scale blend with greedy hill-climbing or direct numerical optimization against the competition's exact composite metric, the one lever with a documented gain (+0.003 on a comparable Kaggle task) of the same magnitude as the gap to rank 1; (2) add a rank-preserving, exact-quota-constrained calibration layer (Sood-style isotonic-plus-sum projection, or a PU-learning-style known-prior correction) as a distinct, low-cost post-processing step beyond Platt/isotonic, since the shape of s(x) is not what this class of method improves — only the threshold/marginal is; (3) cautiously pilot fold-wise, confidence-filtered pseudo-labeling only after re-reading this specific Zindi competition's rules, given Zindi's inconsistent historical stance on test-set usage. Conversely, three paths are probably not worth testing: a deep-learning replacement or even a heavyweight hybrid architecture (NODE, GrowNet), since the signal's confirmed additive-Gaussian structure is precisely the regime where the GAM-vs-NN literature shows the smallest, often non-significant neural advantage; semi-parametric single-index estimators (Klein-Spady, Ichimura, Han MRC), since they pay an efficiency cost to relax an assumption (unknown noise distribution) that is already resolved in this problem's favor; and chasing "new" boosting-library features from 2024-2026, since XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost's recent releases are documented engineering work (memory, GPU, I/O) with no claimed or measured accuracy gain on additive, rare-target signals. The deepest unresolved question this research surfaced is not technical but adversarial: if the top of the leaderboard reflects oracle exploitation rather than superior modeling, the remaining 0.0027 logloss / 0.003 AUC gap may be structurally unreachable by honest means, and the pipeline's near-optimality should be read as a success condition, not a failure to fix.
