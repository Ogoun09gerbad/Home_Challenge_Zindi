# Three new angles confirm the same dead end

The third research pass — loss correction under known Gaussian noise, noisy ranking (Thurstone/Plackett-Luce/TrueSkill), and two-level stacking — **confirms from three independent angles** the literature gap already identified in the two previous reports: no published theory knows how to recover an additive score function s(x) when the selection of the positive label depends on an individual's **relative rank** among all other draws of the same day, rather than on a fixed threshold or an individual exposure probability. The structural diagnosis that emerges, clear and repeated across all three research notes, is that the entire selection-bias correction literature reviewed — Heckman, WESML, SCAR/SAR PU-learning, position-bias LTR — treats selection as an individual probability function p(x) or an absolute threshold on s(x)+ε, never as a rank competition between individuals in the same draw; it is precisely this "cross-individual" dependence that is missing everywhere. Two concrete but unquantified leads nonetheless emerge, and have never yet been tested empirically by the team: empirical-Bayes shrinkage with known variance (James-Stein/Tweedie, in the spirit of Gu & Koenker 2023) applied as post-processing to already well-fitted predictions, and Feature-Weighted Linear Stacking (Sill et al. 2009, 2nd place in the Netflix Prize), which makes the blend weight vary with the features instead of using a fixed global weight. Neither is documented with a quantified gain transferable to the targeted gap (0.003-0.005 composite score), and their compute cost is low compared with the previous attempts at massive retraining. Honesty nevertheless requires acknowledging that this third research pass adds, at bottom, only one more confirmation — not a breakthrough — to a conclusion already stated twice: the gap to rank 1 may remain out of reach of any legitimate modeling.

## Thurstone, Plackett-Luce and TrueSkill solve the inverse of our problem

The Thurstone model — a latent score corrupted by additive noise, estimated from comparisons — is, on paper, the exact mathematical abstraction of this challenge's generating mechanism: a score plus Gaussian noise. But its entire estimation literature, from Hunter (2004) to Negahban et al. (2017), including TrueSkill, solves a structurally different problem: estimating a scalar score per item from **many repeated comparisons of the same item**, not a regression function s(x) from a single noisy realization per individual ([Heterogeneous Thurstone Preference Models, AAAI 2020](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/5860)). TrueSkill itself formalizes each game as a latent Gaussian "performance" around the player's skill score, inferred by message passing on a factor graph — but it is a framework with repeated draws per player, and no "contextual TrueSkill" extension coupling covariates x with a nonparametric score function could be located despite a mature twenty-year literature ([Wikipedia TrueSkill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TrueSkill)). The absence of such an extension in such a well-documented subfield is a fairly strong signal that the exercise has simply never been formalized, rather than a search oversight. The formal link between Plackett-Luce and a TrueSkill-type Thurstonian-Gaussian model was confirmed as early as 2009 ([ICML 2009, Bayesian PL](https://icml.cc/imls/conferences/2009/papers/347.pdf)), which strengthens the conceptual proximity without closing the methodological gap: our problem is the inverse — a single noisy observation per item, where identification must go through the cross-structure of binary labels across individuals, not through repetition.

The neighboring field of "top-k learning-to-rank" in recommendation does have empirically validated, quantified selection-bias correction methods — but they correct an **unknown** selection mechanism, estimated via exposure propensities (Heckman-LTR, Causal Likelihood Decomposition), typically in click systems where it is unknown why a document was or was not seen ([Ovaisi et al., WSDM 2020, arXiv 2001.11358](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.11358) ; [CLD, arXiv 2204.00815](https://arxiv.org/html/2204.00815v2)). Our case is in this respect simpler in theory — the selection mechanism (exact 15% quota, known Gaussian noise) is known analytically, not something to estimate — which makes these propensity-based tools over-parameterized and ill-suited: they try to estimate what we already know. The closest lead identified in all of biased-selection PU-learning, Teisseyre, Martens, Bekker & Davis (AISTATS 2025, "Learning from biased positive-unlabeled data via threshold calibration"), does handle the case of biased non-SCAR positive labels, but its full text remained inaccessible (404 on the PDF) and its abstract does not specify whether the labeling mechanism there is an exact rank/quota rather than an x-dependent propensity ([PMLR v258](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v258/teisseyre25a.html)). The net result, repeated in the two previous reports under other terminologies (Cosslett, Imbens, Sood) and confirmed here under that of noisy ranking and LTR, is that **any method exploiting this gap would be an original construction**, not the application of a published result.

## Known continuous noise flies under the radar of "label noise learning"

The ML literature on loss correction under known noise (Natarajan 2013, Patrini 2017, and their 2024-2026 follow-ups) remains almost entirely centered on **discrete class noise** — one or two scalar corruption rates, or a transition matrix between classes — never on a known continuous distribution added to a latent score before quota thresholding ([NeurIPS 2013](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/5073-learning-with-) ; [CVPR 2017](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2017/papers/Patrini_Making_Deep_Neural_CVPR_2017_paper.pdf)). Even the most recent work found in the 2024-2026 window — an instance-dependent noise model at ICLR 2026 ([mlanthology.org](https://mlanthology.org/iclr/2026/aydn2026iclr-resurfacing/)), or D&C-Net, which estimates a Gaussian mixture over the distribution of *losses* rather than over the latent score itself ([arXiv 2505.13342](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.13342v1)) — does not address the precise s(x)+ε setting with ε analytically known Gaussian. This applied origin explains the divergence: the "noisy labels" community was shaped by human annotation error (crowdsourcing), an inherently discrete phenomenon, whereas our mechanism is continuous measurement noise, closer to the latent-variable econometrics already covered (probit/Tobit) or to empirical-Bayes decision theory than to deep learning on noisy labels.

A classic econometric result cited in this research directly sheds light on the underlying question: if both the probit link and the Gaussian noise are correctly specified — which is empirically confirmed for this pipeline — then the standard MLE already in use attains full asymptotic efficiency (Cramér-Rao bound), and no additional loss correction can bring an asymptotic gain, only possible small-sample gains or gains under residual misspecification (indirect source, Chesher 1983, not read in full text). Consistent with this, no source found compares a loss-correction method against an already well-specified MLE on a latent-score + Gaussian-noise DGP: all quantified gains available in the literature (for example +1.2 to +1.5 accuracy points under 20-40% symmetric class noise, [arXiv 2510.10208](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.10208)) compare against an **uncorrected** cross-entropy, a baseline one or two orders of magnitude wider than our target gap of 0.003-0.005, and therefore not transferable.

The most relevant lead found for this exact sub-problem comes not from deep learning but from the empirical-Bayes decision theory of "compound decisions": Gu & Koenker (Econometrica 2023, "Invidious Comparisons") pose precisely the problem of selecting the most meritorious group from noisy scalar scores of heterogeneous precision, via the Kiefer & Wolfowitz NPMLE ([arXiv 2012.12550](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.12550)). Laird & Louis (1989) show, in a Gaussian setting, that empirical-Bayes ranks differ from raw ranks through a shrinkage effect toward a common value, whose magnitude depends on the precision of each measurement — the most directly relevant "denoising before ranking" mechanism identified in all of this research ([Laird & Louis, PDF](https://faculty.washington.edu/heagerty/Courses/b571/handouts/LairdLouis-EBRankingMethods.pdf)). Neither the Kiefer-Wolfowitz NPMLE nor Laird-Louis shrinkage generally assumes a **known parametric** noise distribution — they estimate it nonparametrically — which suggests that the present case (exactly Gaussian noise, known σ) should in theory allow an exact parametric shrinkage (Tweedie / James-Stein formula) that is even more efficient. But no source in this research quantifies that gain for a top-quota selection setting on a binary variable — neither the Gu & Koenker PDFs nor the applications to US kidney dialysis centers (which probably contain those figures) could be read in full text.

## Two-level stacking is redundant with the already-abandoned hill-climbing

With a pool of only 2-3 already-correlated base models (LightGBM, CatBoost, GAM, all approximating the same additive probit score), the literature and practitioners consistently converge on a zero or marginal gain from two-level stacking relative to an already-optimized linear blend. A controlled synthetic experiment illustrates the mechanism: with diversified models, stacking beats the best single model by about 1 point, but with three mutually correlated tree ensembles, the stacked result stays within the noise of the best single model (0.835 vs 0.834) — the explanation offered is that models that agree too much leave nothing for the meta-model to exploit ([Does Stacking Beat Your Best Model?](https://towardsdatascience.com/does-stacking-beat-your-best-model/)). A documented counter-example points the same way with a competition profile close to ours (Zillow real-estate regression, weak and noisy signal): 3+-level stacking (StackNet) reached ~0.0647 on the leaderboard there, versus ~0.0645 for simpler 2-level stacking — the additional complexity did not help ([kaz-Anova/StackNet, issue #47](https://github.com/kaz-Anova/StackNet/issues/47)). An older academic comparison (Džeroski & Ženko, 2002) confirms that several state-of-the-art stacking methods at best tie with simply selecting the best classifier by cross-validation — the equivalent of the hill-climbing already tested and abandoned in this project ([Stacking with multi-response model trees](https://kt.ijs.si/bernard/publication/dzeroski-2002-stacking/)). An algebraic argument closes the loop: a logistic regression on only 2-3 nearly collinear probit scores has very few additional degrees of freedom to exploit compared with a continuous weight search already performed — which directly explains why no distinct gain is reported for this precise case. All successful stacking precedents found (Otto Group, Chris Deotte's 3-level stacking on an April 2025 Kaggle Playground with 75 models selected from ~500 trials) involve tens to hundreds of genuinely diversified base models — a context not transferable to a pool of 2-3 already-similar models ([NVIDIA Developer Blog, Grandmaster Pro-Tip](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/grandmaster-pro-tip-winning-first-place-in-a-kaggle-competition-with-stacking-using-cuml/)).

The only lead that stands out as non-redundant with the fixed-weight blend and the hill-climbing already tested is Feature-Weighted Linear Stacking (FWLS, Sill, Takacs, Mackey, Lin, 2009, from the 2nd-place Netflix Prize solution): the blend remains linear, but the base-model weights are themselves linear functions of meta-features, so the optimal weight varies across regions of feature space instead of being a global constant ([arXiv 0911.0460](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/0911.0460)). This is a mechanism that neither the fixed-weight blend nor greedy hill-climbing can capture, since both search for a single global weight. Its neural-network extension (NN-Stacking) generalizes the same principle ([arXiv 1906.09735](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1906.09735)). However, no gain magnitude could be extracted outside the Netflix collaborative-filtering setting, and no 2024-2026 Kaggle write-up found documents a "blend saturates with correlated models → FWLS or stacking still finds a gain" case on a pool of comparable size (≤5 models) — the lead is structurally promising but unquantified.

## Two low-cost, untested leads with no quantified guarantee

This third research pass surfaces exactly two concrete leads that the team has not yet tested empirically, and which stand apart from previous attempts (Platt/isotonic recalibrations, EM-Tobit, hill-climbing) by their low compute cost: neither requires retraining the base models, only post-processing of predictions already produced. The first, James-Stein/Tweedie-style empirical-Bayes shrinkage with known variance, would shrink individual predictions toward a common value according to each one's own uncertainty — a principle distinct from the already-abandoned EM-Tobit, which re-estimated the score function s(x) itself rather than applying post-hoc shrinkage to already well-fitted predictions. The second, FWLS, would replace the constant global blend weight with a weight that varies with the original features. In both cases, no source consulted in this research provides a bound or quantified result allowing one to anticipate whether the gain would reach the targeted order of magnitude (0.003-0.005 composite) — their appeal lies in their relative novelty and low trial cost, not in any proof of gain.

## Conclusion

Three independent research passes, conducted under three different terminologies (noisy ranking/Thurstone, loss correction under known noise, multi-level stacking), converge on the same structural diagnosis that the two previous reports had already established: the gap is not an isolated search oversight but a fundamental limit of the entire accessible biased-selection literature, which systematically models selection as an individual probability or an absolute threshold, never as a relative rank depending simultaneously on the scores of all other individuals in the same draw. Any method that directly exploited this relative structure would therefore be an original construction by the team, not the application of a published result — which changes the nature of the risk: a trial of empirical-Bayes shrinkage or FWLS is cheap to test, but carries the theoretical guarantee of no external source. Given the number of leads already exhausted without measurable gain on this highly optimized pipeline — recalibrations, EM-Tobit, hill-climbing over ~18 models, and now this review extended to three additional angles — honesty requires not presenting empirical-Bayes shrinkage or FWLS as likely levers for closing the 0.003-0.005 gap to rank 1, but as the last two legitimate, low-cost trials before accepting, as the two previous reports already suggested, that this gap may remain out of reach of honest modeling alone.
